# Turning Branchline into your UC application

This guide helps you describe the project truthfully and well. It does not write anything for you, and it shouldn't. Reviewers want your voice and real details.

## First, a timing check

For fall 2027 entry, the UC filing period runs October 1 to November 30, 2026. You can only describe what has actually happened by the time you submit. If you have already built the project, great. If a workshop or other plan is still in the future, don't list it as done. Describe the project as it really stands, then keep the impact log going.

If you are applying later, you have more time. A semester of real teaching, real feedback, and real contributors is far stronger than a repo with a long README.

## The big idea

A certification says you learned something. A project that helps other people says what you do with what you learn. Admissions readers look for initiative, impact, growth, and what you did with the opportunities you had. The tool is the backdrop, not the story.

Strong versions of this project have three things:

1. **You built something real.** Branchline, published and working.
2. **Other people used it.** A workshop, a club session, a classmate who got unstuck.
3. **You learned from how it went.** Feedback you acted on, a bug someone else found, a design you changed.

## Where it goes in the application

**Activities and awards.** You can list up to 20 activities, and descriptions are short, so every word has to carry weight. Pick the category that fits what you actually did. Building and sharing the project is usually an extracurricular activity. If you taught workshops for free in your community, that may be a separate entry under volunteer and community service. Check the category descriptions in the application. If your Git certification fits an awards or honors entry, list it there. Otherwise mention it briefly in the project description.

**Personal insight questions (PIQs).** You answer 4 of 8, up to 350 words each, and all eight are weighted equally. Choose the questions where your real stories fit, not the ones that sound most impressive. Two cautions:

- Don't use a PIQ to repeat your activities list. Use it for the meaning behind the activity.
- Reviewers read the words you type. Don't count on them visiting a link, so put what matters in the text.

Prompts this project could support, depending on your story (check the exact wording on UC's site, because it can change):

- **Leadership**: if you organized a workshop or led a group of contributors
- **Greatest talent or skill**: if the story is how you built the skill and what you do with it
- **Academic subject that inspires you**: if computer science genuinely does, and you can say why in your own terms
- **Making your school or community better**: if you taught others or fixed a real problem for them
- **Beyond what's in your application**: for something that doesn't fit elsewhere

## Activity description template

Keep it concrete, in active voice, and true. Fill the brackets only with real facts.

> **Name:** Branchline: free Git learning tool, creator and instructor
>
> **Description:** Built an interactive website that teaches Git by drawing commit history as a transit map; published it on GitHub Pages. Taught [N] students in [a workshop at ___] and revised the lessons based on their feedback. [Other people] contributed [N] fixes through pull requests.

Cut anything you can't back up. If you worked alone, don't say "team." If it's 14 people, don't say "dozens." Numbers from your [impact log](impact-log.md) beat adjectives.

**Hours per week and weeks per year** should come from the log. Estimate honestly if you didn't track early on.

## Questions to ask yourself before writing a PIQ

Write quick, messy answers first. These produce the specific details that make an essay sound like you.

- What was the first thing that confused me about Git? Who or what finally made it click?
- Tell the story of one bug I couldn't fix. What did I try, and what finally worked?
- Describe one person in the workshop who struggled. What did I change after that?
- What did I do differently the second time I taught it?
- What surprised me about explaining something I thought I understood?
- What do I care about that this project is really a way of acting on?
- If the project disappeared tomorrow, what would I still have learned?

## Things to avoid

- **Inflating.** Applications are signed statements. Inaccurate claims can cost you an offer.
- **Padding with tool names.** "Used Git, GitHub, Actions, Pages, Node" reads like a list. Say what you did with them.
- **Pretending it's bigger than it is.** A small project that taught six people well is a good story. Say so.
- **Letting someone else write it.** UC wants your own words. Getting feedback is fine. Having something written for you is not.

## Quick checklist before you submit

- [ ] Every number in my entries comes from my impact log
- [ ] The repo is public, has a clear README, and the live site works
- [ ] I replaced all `YOUR-USERNAME` and `YOUR NAME` placeholders
- [ ] My activity descriptions use verbs and specifics, not adjectives
- [ ] My PIQs tell stories the activities list can't
- [ ] A teacher or counselor read my drafts

Verify all deadlines, limits, and prompt wording on the official UC admissions site before submitting.
